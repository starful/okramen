Tokyo has enough ramen shops that you could plan a different bowl for every meal of a month-long trip and still miss half the good ones. This is a single, deliberately paced day for visitors who want a genuine cross-section of styles — tsukemen, shio, chicken paitan, W-soup shoyu, and a Michelin-recognized tantanmen — without spending the whole day just riding trains. Treat the times below as a loose frame, not a reservation; almost none of these shops take bookings, and queues are part of the experience.

## Before you start: a few ground rules

*   **Pace yourself.** Five bowls in one day means small portions and shared plates if you're traveling with someone. Order a regular size at the first two or three stops, then decide how you feel.
*   **Cash helps.** Many counter-style shops still run on ticket vending machines (*shokkenki*) and don't take cards. Keep ¥1,000 notes and coins on hand.
*   **Confirm hours before you go.** Ramen shops in Tokyo close for random days, run out of broth early, or shift hours seasonally. Screenshot the Google Maps listing for each stop the morning of your trip — this itinerary is editorial guidance, not a booking service.
*   **Weekday mornings are calmer.** If you can do this on a weekday rather than a weekend, queues at the popular stops shrink noticeably.

## Stop 1 (late morning, ~10:30–11:00): Tsukemen in Shinjuku

Start at [Fuunji Shinjuku](/ramen/fuunji_shinjuku_en), one of Tokyo's most consistently recommended tsukemen counters, tucked near Shinjuku's south side. Tsukemen — noodles served separately from a concentrated dipping broth — is a great opener because the portions are noodle-forward and the broth is sipped rather than drunk in full, so you won't feel over-full for the rest of the day.

**Order:** the standard tsukemen set. When you finish the noodles, ask for *soup-wari* (a lighter broth poured in to dilute the dipping sauce) so you can drink the remainder like a soup — it's part of the ritual, not an upsell.

**Getting there:** JR Shinjuku Station, a short walk from the south exit. Arrive close to opening if you want to avoid the lunch-rush line that builds by 11:30.

## Stop 2 (early afternoon, ~13:00): Shio ramen in Harajuku

Take the Yamanote Line one stop to Harajuku/Sangubashi for [Afuri Harajuku](/ramen/afuri_harajuku_en), known for a citrus-inflected shio (salt) broth that's noticeably lighter than the tonkotsu and shoyu bowls most visitors expect. It's a deliberate palate reset after the richer tsukemen dip.

**Order:** the shio ramen — don't add extra oil or toppings on your first taste. Let the yuzu aromatics come through before customizing.

**Nearby:** this is a natural place to fold in Harajuku/Omotesando sightseeing between stops 2 and 3, since you'll want at least two hours before your next bowl.

## Stop 3 (late afternoon, ~16:00): Chicken paitan in Ginza

Head to [Ginza Kagari](/ramen/ginza_kagari_en) for a chicken-based bowl in the middle of Tokyo's upscale shopping district. It's a smaller counter than the other stops on this course, so expect a short wait even at an off-peak hour — that's normal, not a bad sign.

**Order:** the shop's signature bowl on the menu board; there usually isn't much need to customize here, the chicken broth is built to be balanced as-is.

**Getting there:** Tokyo Metro to Ginza Station. This stop doubles as a good excuse to explore Ginza's department stores and backstreets before the evening rush.

## Stop 4 (evening, ~19:00): W-soup shoyu back in Shinjuku

Ride back toward Shinjuku for [Konjiki Hototogisu](/ramen/konjiki_hototogisu_en), a shop that built its reputation on a "W-soup" (double soup) shoyu broth blending chicken and seafood stocks — it's a good showcase of how far shoyu ramen has evolved past the simple clear broths of the mid-20th century.

**Order:** classic shoyu ramen. Taste the broth on its own for a minute before adding any table condiments so you can pick out the seafood-stock layer underneath the soy sauce.

**Timing note:** dinner-hour queues here can run 20–40 minutes on weekends. If you're tired by this point in the day, this is the stop to skip and fold into a future trip — the other four still make a complete, satisfying day.

## Stop 5 (late night, ~22:00): Michelin-recognized tantanmen in Toshima

For the ambitious, finish the day at [Nakiryu](/ramen/nakiryu_en) in Toshima (Otsuka/Ikebukuro area), one of the small number of ramen shops in Japan to hold a Michelin star. It's a short ride on the Yamanote or a taxi from Shinjuku.

**Order:** whatever the counter is currently known for — ask staff or point at the most-ordered item if the menu isn't in English. Portions here tend to be modest, which is exactly what you want after four earlier bowls.

**Reality check:** Michelin-listed ramen shops sell out nightly. If it's closed or the line is too long by the time you arrive, treat stop 4 as your finale and save this one for a dedicated visit — don't force a sixth bowl into an already full day.

## Suggested pacing at a glance

| Time | Area | Style | Shop |

| 10:30–11:00 | Shinjuku | Tsukemen | Fuunji Shinjuku |
| 13:00 | Harajuku | Shio | Afuri Harajuku |
| 16:00 | Ginza | Chicken paitan | Ginza Kagari |
| 19:00 | Shinjuku | W-soup shoyu | Konjiki Hototogisu |
| 22:00 | Toshima | Tantanmen (Michelin) | Nakiryu |

If five stops feels like too much, cut it to three: Fuunji Shinjuku for tsukemen, Ginza Kagari for a lighter chicken bowl, and Konjiki Hototogisu to close the night — that alone is a satisfying, varied day of Tokyo ramen.
