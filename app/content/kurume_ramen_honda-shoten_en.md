## Why Honda-shoten Matters to Kurume's Ramen Story

Kurume is where tonkotsu ramen was born, and Honda-shoten is one of the shops still cooking the way the style demands: a master broth (継ぎ足し, tsugitashi) that is never fully emptied, only topped up day after day, year after year, so the pot itself becomes a kind of living archive of every batch that came before it. This isn't a marketing story pasted onto a modern shop — it's the actual mechanics of how the broth gets its density. The result is a soup that reads darker, muddier, and more aggressively porky than the cleaner, lighter tonkotsu you'll find in most Hakata shops thirty minutes north. If you've only had Ichiran or Ippudo-style bowls, this is the rougher, more traditional cousin, and it's worth the detour specifically because it tastes like Kurume, not like a export-friendly version of it.

## What to Order

Stick with the plain tonkotsu ramen on your first visit — it's the dish the master broth exists to serve, and ordering anything more elaborate the first time around just gets in the way of tasting what the shop actually does well. The noodles are thin, straight, and cooked firm; if there's a noodle-firmness option on the ticket machine or verbal order, "kata" (firm) or the default setting is the traditional choice here, since Kurume-style bowls are built around noodles that hold up in a thick, mineral-heavy broth rather than go soft in a thin one. Kaedama (noodle refills) are the standard move if you're still hungry after the first portion — order it while there's still a bit of soup left, since the point is to stretch the broth across two noodle servings, not to eat a second full bowl. If chashu, kikurage (wood ear mushroom), or a soft-boiled egg are offered as toppings, they're worth adding, but they're supporting cast, not the reason to visit. Skip anything that feels like it's diluting the broth's intensity, like a "light" or "assari" version if one exists — that defeats the purpose of coming to a master-broth shop in the first place.

## Reading the Queue and Hours Reality

Local, working-class Kurume ramen shops like this one tend to run tighter, more irregular hours than tourist-facing counters in central Fukuoka — expect a lunch service window and a separate evening window, with a mid-afternoon closure in between, and don't be surprised if the shop closes early once the day's broth or noodle allotment runs out. This is common practice at shops that make a point of not padding output with shortcuts, and it means showing up at 8pm expecting a full menu can be a real gamble. Always verify current hours on Google Maps or the shop's listed social accounts before you make the trip, since single-owner or family-run counters like this adjust hours seasonally and don't always update signage. Queue-wise, expect the heaviest pressure around the core lunch hour (roughly 12:00–13:00) on weekends, when local regulars and ramen-touring visitors overlap; arriving right at opening or in the later stretch of the lunch window usually means walking straight to a stool. Seating is typically counter-style and limited, so solo travelers and pairs will move through the line faster than larger groups.

## Getting There from the Nearest Station

Honda-shoten sits in the city of Kurume, which is most efficiently reached from Fukuoka (Hakata) via the JR Kagoshima Main Line or the Nishitetsu Tenjin Omuta Line, both of which land at Kurume-area stations in well under an hour. From whichever Kurume station you arrive at, this is a walkable-to-short-taxi neighborhood shop rather than something directly attached to the station concourse — plug the coordinates (33.3195, 130.5108) into Google or Apple Maps before you leave the platform, since Kurume's residential blocks aren't always intuitively signed in English. If you're combining this with other Kurume ramen landmarks (the city has several historically significant shops within a few kilometers of each other), it's worth mapping out a short walking or taxi loop rather than treating this as a single there-and-back trip from Fukuoka city center.

## Who Should Make the Trip — and Who Can Skip It

This shop rewards travelers who already know tonkotsu ramen and want to taste the style's origin point rather than a polished, tourist-optimized version of it. If you're chasing the deepest, funkiest, most traditional expression of Kurume tonkotsu and don't mind a shop that looks and feels like a genuine neighborhood spot rather than a design-forward flagship, this belongs on your itinerary. It's also a solid pick for travelers who've already done the big-name Fukuoka tonkotsu chains and want to understand what those chains are actually descended from. On the other hand, if you're short on time in Fukuoka and only have room for one or two ramen stops, the convenience math favors shops closer to Hakata or Tenjin — the trip out to Kurume is a deliberate pilgrimage, not a quick detour. Travelers sensitive to very rich, heavy pork-bone broths, or anyone looking for a lighter or more modern take on tonkotsu, will likely find this bowl more intense than they want, and should look toward lighter Hakata-style shops instead.
